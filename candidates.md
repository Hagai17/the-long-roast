# Candidates

Fourteen candidate sentences were drafted and duelled pairwise against the
judge's criteria, in order: (1) a one-sentence satirical roast of lawmakers
who regulate AI, crypto or the internet without being able to convert a file
to PDF; (2) a real turn — authority set up, then undercut by the file they
cannot convert; (3) aim kept on lawmakers and the rules they write, about
power and competence rather than random abuse; (4) clean in one pass,
quotable, punch on the last few words; (5) a specific, unexpected detail over
a worn tech-illiteracy cliché. Every candidate was verified by script to be
exactly one sentence and at most 320 characters.

## Winner (177 characters)

The lawmaker who wants to license every large model spent forty minutes
converting one memo, succeeded only in password-protecting a blank page, and
then subpoenaed the printer.

## Runner-up (187 characters)

After six hearings on cybersecurity, the committee chair converted the final
report by printing all 412 pages, scanning them back in sideways, and calling
the result 'the searchable PDF.'

## Why the winner won

Both finalists set up real authority and undercut it with a conversion
failure, but the winner turns the lawmaker's own power into the punchline.
Its setup is maximal — a man who would license every large model — and its
turn is a forty-minute conversion attempt whose only product is a
password-protected blank page. The last three words, "subpoenaed the
printer," land the joke exactly where criterion 4 wants it: he answers a
competence failure by aiming his legislative power at the nearest machine,
so the roast stays on power and competence (criterion 3) rather than random
abuse. The detail is specific and unexpected (criterion 5) — no "what is a
PDF" cliché — and the sentence reads clean in one pass. The runner-up's
ironic "searchable PDF" is a strong closer, but its setup (six hearings) is
thinner authority and its failure is a staff-room mishap rather than the
lawmaker personally wielding power he has not earned.

The rest of the field fell in earlier rounds: renaming the file from .docx
to .pdf, photographing the monitor for the IT desk, faxing the press release
to itself, and uploading a speech to a free converter site all had specific
details but softer final-word punches; the screenshot-and-taskbar-crop and
nephew-by-email entries rambled past their turns; and the entries leaning on
the "does not have the PDF button" and public-library gags read as worn
tech-illiteracy clichés against criterion 5.
